## The thesis (rough)

There's a skill people talk about constantly — "be willing to say I don't know" — that gets credit it doesn't deserve. Admitting ignorance is half the skill. The full skill is admitting it *and* doing something about it: scoping the gap, asking the right person, learning fast enough that you don't have to admit the same thing twice. People who only do the first half look humble but never actually grow. People who do both are how teams compound.

## What to flesh out in future recordings

- The two failure modes: (1) won't admit, fakes their way through; (2) admits but doesn't follow through — both produce the same end result
- Why the "AND" is the load-bearing part — the admitting is cheap signaling, the follow-through is the actual skill
- How to spot it in interviews: someone who says "I don't know — here's how I'd find out" vs someone who just says "I don't know"
- The cultural prerequisite: teams that punish ignorance create the first failure mode; teams that reward shallow humility create the second
- Personal angle: where I've gotten this right and where I've gotten it wrong
- The connection to the three pillars in `evaluating-judgement` — this sits at the seam between technical skill and motivation
- Counterintuitive: the most senior people you've worked with say "I don't know" *more* often than juniors, not less. Why.
