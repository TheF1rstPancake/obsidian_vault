## Key claims

- **T-shaped people**: broad competence across domains plus deep expertise in one. Enables cross-functional conversations while providing specialized value.
- **Missionaries vs mercenaries**: assess whether candidates believe in the mission or are purely financially motivated.
- **Execution focus over philosophy**: prioritize doers; warns against candidates more interested in reading and theorizing than working.
- **Test real work outside the stated skillset**: engineering interviews should include product/design questions, not just coding trivia.
- **Three-day working interviews** as a proxy for actual collaboration.
- **Soft factors weighted equally** with skillset: work style, decision-making, pacing, motivation source.

## My notes

Chen has good judgement himself but he sees it purely through a technical lens. For deeply evaluating someone's *current* technical skills, T-shaped is probably right. But it discredits someone who has the *potential* to be T-shaped — because they are motivated to learn and know how to prioritize what to learn, and one of the skills they possess is self-teaching. If you only look for people who are already T-shaped, you miss the opportunity to find the people who can *become* T-shaped. That's the version of the framework that actually scales: hire for the meta-skill (the three pillars) and let the T-shape develop on the job. Hiring for the T-shape directly is hiring the output instead of the input.
